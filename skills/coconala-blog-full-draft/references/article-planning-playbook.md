# Reader-facing article planning playbook

## Role

Use this playbook before drafting a Coconala blog article. The goal is not to summarize the product page. The goal is to make the reader feel, "This is about my problem," understand why the service may help, and feel safe enough to consider the product.

## Planning output

Before drafting, create a compact internal plan with:

- target reader;
- reader pain or hesitation;
- ideal state after reading;
- product value from visible facts only;
- safe proof from visible facts only;
- missing details that must not be invented;
- title candidates when the user has not fixed a title;
- three two-line heading candidates;
- CTA direction.

Do not paste this internal plan into Coconala unless the user asks for it. Use it to make the final article stronger.

## Eight-part flow

Adapt this flow to the shorter Coconala blog format:

1. Name the reader's worry in plain language.
2. Show the relatable stuck point without blaming the reader.
3. Introduce the service or method as a possible way forward.
4. Describe the safe change the reader can expect from the visible product facts.
5. Explain why the approach helps in beginner-friendly language.
6. Add safe trust support from visible facts only; if none exists, skip rather than invent.
7. Answer likely concerns such as difficulty, fit, flow, or uncertainty.
8. Lead naturally to the product URL.

## Title rules

If the user supplies an adopted title, preserve it exactly.

If no title is fixed, propose and choose a title that combines:

- before state or reader pain;
- method, product category, or service promise from visible facts;
- desired next state.

Prefer titles that sound specific and human. Avoid hype, guaranteed outcomes, sales counts, ranking claims, rare/limited claims, or expert credentials unless visible on the live product page.

## Heading rules

Coconala blog headings must remain three headings, each as two standalone lines.

Make headings reader-facing. Do not use structural labels such as `悩みの代弁`, `Before`, `After`, `CTA`, or `不安への回答`.

Good headings:

- name a concrete worry;
- hint at the next step;
- show the service fit;
- feel warm and specific.

Weak headings:

- sound like a generic lesson;
- expose the outline mechanics;
- promise results not visible on the product page;
- use stiff sales language.

## Missing-information compensation

When useful information is missing, compensate by:

- using visible product facts;
- making the article about the reader's uncertainty instead of fake results;
- using general process language such as "整理する", "向き合う", "確認する", or "一歩進める";
- adding a private note for the user if a detail would improve the article.

Never invent:

- reviews;
- ratings;
- sales numbers;
- platinum rank or awards;
- client outcomes;
- qualifications;
- urgency, scarcity, or guarantees;
- exact time, price conditions, or deadlines not visible.

## Tone

Use warm, concrete Japanese. Keep the reader as the hero.

Use "私" for negative or learning moments. Use "あなた" or the reader's situation for positive future-facing moments. Do not shame the reader, overstate danger, or pressure them.

## CTA

The CTA should feel like the next natural step after the article. Mention what the reader can clarify, organize, or receive by opening the product page. Put the live product URL on its own final line.

Do not include price unless the workflow specifically requires visible price facts and they are safe for the target platform. Do not make the CTA louder than the article.
